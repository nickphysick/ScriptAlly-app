Synopsis
What the two toggles actually decide
  • Primary buttons in slate. +New, Upgrade and Log query stop being near-black and take the band's colour. This is the stronger of the two ideas: it makes the band read as the app's chrome colour rather than one page's decoration, and near-black was never a brand colour — it was the absence of one. Watch the sidebar's Upgrade button, which is the largest area of it.
  • Nav and breadcrumb text in slate. The whole left rail and the top bar go cool while the working surface stays warm. It reads as a deliberate two-material system — cool chrome, warm paper — and it is the more consequential change, because it moves the app's overall temperature rather than three buttons.
  • Burgundy is untouched in both. Status dots, avatars, the selected row's spine, Record response, the To-do count, the overdue state. That matters: if slate takes the chrome and burgundy keeps the content, each colour has one job. If slate also crept into content, the app would have two competing accent colours and no hierarchy.
  • The thing to judge is the sidebar. With both toggles on, the rail is cool text on warm parchment, which can read as elegant or as slightly off-register depending on how much warmth the parchment carries. Toggle nav alone first, then both.
  • Both buttons white with black text — the default now. On sage this works, and it works for a reason: the band is light enough that white still reads as raised rather than as a hole. Note what it costs: the two buttons become the same object, distinguished only by the primary's slight shadow and its weight. That is a real hierarchy but a quiet one, and Export is the button you want people to press less often.
  • The split option keeps them white-and-outlined — filled white primary, transparent ghost with a rim in the band's own ink. It is the same relationship the page uses everywhere else: filled means primary, outlined means secondary. Toggle between them with your eye on which button you reach for first.
  • Band colour is now separate from band treatment, so all six combinations are reachable. Sage solid is the `#c9d2c7` you picked earlier, with ink-dark labels; sage accent is the same colour as a 2px or 5px line. The accent versions read their primary from whatever the button toggle is set to, so a sage accent with slate buttons is a legitimate pairing to look at.
  • Sage solid against sage card headers is the one to check carefully — it is the stripe risk I flagged earlier. With the cards' sage sitting a few pixels below a sage band, the pane can read as banded rather than composed. Slate does not have that problem, which is a real argument for it that has nothing to do with which colour is nicer.
  • The band as a white ground with a slate accent — the first toggle set. White + slate rule puts a 2px slate line along the band's top edge; thick makes it 5px. Both keep the label and buttons on white, so the primary returns to a filled slate pill and the ghost returns to its normal rim — no inversion needed, which removes the one genuinely awkward part of the solid version.
  • What you gain and lose. The accent version is quieter and keeps the page warm from top to bottom; it also stops the band from being the heaviest thing on screen, which matters on a page whose content is meant to be the subject. What it loses is the clear division between chrome and work — the solid band tells you unmistakably where the app ends and the page begins, and a 2px line says the same thing far more faintly. Try both against the sidebar: solid reads as a continuation of the app frame, accent reads as a page element.
  • Card headers in slate — the third toggle. Try Slate and then Slate on tracking only. Two things happen: the pane gains a lot of visual weight, because those bands are large and now dark; and the sage disappears from the product almost entirely, since the card headers are where most of it lives. The glance panel, the journey steps and the status system all use sage — removing it here leaves it stranded in places the writer sees less often.
  • The mixed option exists to test a real idea: that dark is a state, not a style. If Tracking goes dark only when a query is overdue, the colour carries meaning. If every header is dark all the time, it is decoration and the page loses the calm it currently has. Judge it on the overdue query shown here, then imagine it on a query sent yesterday.

    • My read: take the buttons, hold the nav text for a day, and leave the card headers sage. On the band, sage solid with white buttons is a good answer — light enough that white buttons sit on it rather than in it, and warm enough that the page stays one temperature. I would use the split treatment rather than two white buttons, so the band keeps the same filled-means-primary grammar as everything else, but both are defensible and the difference is small. Slate is the chrome colour — the band, the buttons, the frame around the work. The card headers are inside the work, and making them the same colour as the chrome collapses the distinction the band was introduced to create. Sage headers on white cards against slate chrome is a three-part system where each part has a job; slate headers make it two parts and a lot of dark.
