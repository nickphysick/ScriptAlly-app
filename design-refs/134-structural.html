Six structural ways to separate the two rows
The names are now identical in all six — same face, size, weight and ink. The card does the differentiating instead of the type, which is the more durable approach: it survives a long title, a short one, and any future change to the type scale.

A · Hairline — a rule between the rows. Plainest, and it says only these are two things. Safe, and slightly administrative.
B · Enclosure tint — the manuscript half sits in a recessed panel. It borrows the mail metaphor properly: this is the attachment area, and the chips inside it now sit on a surface that explains them. My pick.
C · Nested rail — the book indents under the agent with a soft sage rail, the way an enclosure belongs to a letter. Most expressive of the relationship, but it costs horizontal room and reads slightly like a reply thread.
D · Eyebrows — the words come back, but as 7px marks above each name rather than a label column. Explicit, and it recovers the naming without the width the old labels took.
E · Icon discs — only the leaders differ: the agent's glyph sits in a soft pink disc, the manuscript's stays bare. The lightest touch of the six; whether it is enough depends on how much work you want the icons doing.
F · Two-tone split — the card divides edge to edge, agent on white, manuscript on tint. Strongest and most graphic; it also makes the card look like two stacked cards rather than one, which is the risk.

Also here: Log new query in soft pink with black text, left-aligned above the list — so the page's one creative action sits over the column it adds to, rather than in the band with the destructive verbs. It is the only pink surface on the page, which is exactly the emphasis it deserves.
