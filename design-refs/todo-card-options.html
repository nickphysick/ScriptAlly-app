The task card, five ways

Same three tasks in each — a send, a nudge, a close. Every option keeps the rules you set: fill means task family, glyph means query state; the manuscript sits in a corner; the numeral goes burgundy only where the writer owes the date.

A · Editorial

A magazine grammar: kicker, headline, standfirst, byline. The task type is a coloured word with a dot rather than a pill; the wait is an italic numeral in the standfirst; the agent is the byline with the status beside them. Quietest and most "written".

B · Ticket

A coloured edge carries the family; inside, a two-by-two of labelled facts on a dashed rule, like a stub. Densest — the most tasks per screen — and the most legible at a glance because every fact has a label.

D · Stat-led

The wait is the first thing on the card — a numeral in a paper well on the left — because on a to-do list "how long" is what decides what to open first. Title and date beside it, agent and status beneath. The most triage-friendly.

My order: C, then E. C is the one your reference proved works — the agent block gives the card a centre of gravity, the footer gives the wait a home, and nothing is fighting for the eye. E is the more distinctive and ties card to drawer, but a tinted band on every card is a lot of colour on a page of twenty. A is the most beautiful at three cards and the least scannable at twenty. B wins on density and loses on charm. D is the right answer if you decide "how long" should always come first — which for a to-do list it might.
